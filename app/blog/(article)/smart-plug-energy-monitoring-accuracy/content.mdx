<h2 id="how-it-measures">How a smart plug actually measures power</h2>

Most consumer smart plugs measure current using a **shunt resistor** — a
precision resistor placed in series with the load. By Ohm's Law, the
voltage drop across that resistor is directly proportional to the
current flowing through it, so measuring that small voltage (typically
amplified by a current-sense chip) lets the plug calculate current, and
combined with a voltage reading, calculate power. It's a real,
legitimate electrical engineering technique used throughout industrial
and consumer electronics — the accuracy problem isn't the underlying
method, it's how precisely each specific product implements and
calibrates it.

<h2 id="the-peer-reviewed-test">What a peer-reviewed accuracy test found</h2>

A published study (ScienceDirect) tested five commercially available
energy-monitoring smart plug brands — Belkin, TP-Link, Etekcity,
Emporia, and Sonoff — against a high-accuracy laboratory reference
instrument across a range of load types and variability. The spread in
results was dramatic:

- **Belkin WEMO** had the smallest measured error of any brand tested,
  **never exceeding 1.7%**.
- **The other four brands tested (TP-Link, Etekcity, Emporia, Sonoff)**
  showed maximum energy measurement errors of **54% to 100%** —
  specifically for loads with variable power draw.
- All tested units also showed **reporting delays of 3–6 seconds**, with
  some delays reaching as high as **20 seconds**.

<Callout kind="verified" title="This isn't every unit of every brand, every time">
A single published study testing specific units of five brands isn't a
permanent verdict on every product line from those manufacturers
forever — firmware updates and hardware revisions can change results.
The finding worth taking away isn't "avoid these specific brands," it's
that **massive accuracy variance between smart plug models is real,
documented, and load-dependent** — not a hypothetical concern.
</Callout>

<h2 id="why-variable-loads-break-it">Why variable loads specifically break the measurement</h2>

The study found errors were **highly dependent on load variability**,
with meaningful errors appearing even at variation rates as low as
0.01–0.05 Hz (a load cycling roughly once every 20 to 100 seconds) — a
pattern common in real household electronics like laptops, TVs, and
game consoles that draw power in bursts rather than at one constant
level. A steady, constant load (a space heater, an incandescent bulb) is
the easy case for this kind of sensor; a laptop cycling between idle and
active processing, or a TV alternating between bright and dark scenes,
is a genuinely harder measurement problem — and it's exactly the kind of
device people most want to energy-monitor.

<h2 id="the-hardware-tiers">The hardware tiers that predict accuracy</h2>

Beyond brand-specific results, the underlying hardware tier a plug uses
predicts its likely accuracy range:

| Tier | Typical price | Hardware approach | Typical accuracy on stable loads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry-level | $15–25 | Basic current-sensing IC, estimated power factor | ±8–12% |
| Mid-tier | $30–60 | True-RMS current sensing, factory-calibrated voltage reference | ±3–5% |
| Commercial-grade | $80+ | Dual-shunt sensing, phase-aware monitoring | ±1.5% |

These figures describe **stable-load** performance specifically — per
the peer-reviewed test above, even well-specified units can show
dramatically larger errors once the load itself becomes variable, which
isn't fully captured by a single "accuracy" percentage on a spec sheet.

<h2 id="calibration-drift">Calibration drift: accuracy that degrades after purchase</h2>

A shunt-based current sensor's accuracy depends on a one-time factory
calibration that ties the specific shunt resistor and its readout
electronics together into a stored correction factor. That calibration
can drift over the product's life due to temperature swings,
manufacturing tolerances between individual units (even from the same
production batch), and firmware updates that change the underlying
measurement algorithm — meaning a plug's accuracy isn't necessarily
fixed at the number it shipped with.

<h2 id="what-this-means-practically">What this actually means for your electric bill decisions</h2>

1. **Treat smart plug energy data as directionally useful, not
   bill-accurate** — good for spotting which devices draw the most
   relative power, less reliable for precise dollar-and-cents
   projections.
2. **Be specifically skeptical of "idle power" readings** used to
   justify auto-shutoff automations — standby draw is a small, steady
   signal that's more vulnerable to sensor noise and calibration offset
   than large, active loads.
3. **Expect the largest errors on variable-draw electronics** (laptops,
   TVs, game consoles) rather than steady loads (heaters, simple
   appliances) — the same plug can be meaningfully more accurate on one
   device type than another.
4. **A significantly cheaper or more expensive plug doesn't guarantee
   proportionally different real-world accuracy** — the peer-reviewed
   test found one budget-tier brand (Belkin WEMO) outperforming several
   competitors by a wide margin, so price tier alone isn't a reliable
   predictor.

<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>

Smart plug energy monitoring uses a legitimate, well-understood
measurement technique, but real, independently tested accuracy varies
enormously between brands and — more importantly — between load types
on the very same device. A number showing "$1.27 saved this week" in an
app dashboard reflects a specific hardware and firmware implementation's
best estimate, not a utility-grade measurement, and the peer-reviewed
gap between the best- and worst-performing tested brands (under 2% vs.
over 50-100% error on variable loads) is large enough that it shouldn't
be treated as background-level uncertainty.
