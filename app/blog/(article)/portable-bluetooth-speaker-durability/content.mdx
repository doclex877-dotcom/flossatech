<h2 id="the-two-digit-system">The two-digit system, decoded</h2>

"IP" stands for **Ingress Protection**, a standardized rating system
defined by the International Electrotechnical Commission under **IEC
60529**. Every full IP rating has exactly two digits, and each digit
tests a completely separate thing:

- **First digit (0–6):** protection against solid particles, primarily
  dust. A rating of **6** means the device is fully dust-tight — no dust
  enters under any tested condition.
- **Second digit (0–9):** protection against liquid ingress. Higher
  numbers mean deeper, longer, or more forceful water exposure survived.

Because the two digits test unrelated hazards, a speaker can score well
on one and poorly (or not at all) on the other — which is exactly what
the "X" placeholder is for.

<h2 id="what-the-x-means">What the "X" actually means (and why it's not a red flag)</h2>

A rating like **IPX7** means the manufacturer tested and published only
the water-resistance digit; the dust digit wasn't tested or wasn't
disclosed. That's not automatically a sign of a weaker product — many
speakers are legitimately tested for water resistance only, because
that's the hazard their intended use (poolside, beach, kitchen) actually
presents. It does mean you shouldn't assume dust protection from an
IPX-rated product; if fine sand or grit exposure is a real concern for
how you'll use it, look specifically for a full two-digit rating with a
"6" in the first position instead.

<h2 id="common-ratings-table">Common ratings and what they actually certify</h2>

| Rating | Dust protection | Water protection |
|---|---|---|
| IPX4 | Not tested/rated | Splashing water from any direction — handles sweat and light rain |
| IPX7 | Not tested/rated | Full submersion in up to 1m of fresh water for up to 30 minutes |
| IP67 | Fully dust-tight | Full submersion in up to 1m of fresh water for up to 30 minutes |
| IP68 | Fully dust-tight | Submersion beyond 1m — exact depth and duration set by the manufacturer, not standardized |

<Callout kind="caveat" title="IP68's water spec isn't one fixed number">
Unlike IPX7 and IP67, which specify an exact standardized depth and time
(1 meter, 30 minutes), IP68's water-resistance claim is
manufacturer-defined beyond that baseline — one brand's IP68 speaker
might be rated for 1.5 meters, another's for 3 meters or more. Always
check the specific manufacturer's published depth and duration for an
IP68 product rather than assuming it matches a competitor's.
</Callout>

<h2 id="the-jbl-flip-example">A real example of the industry moving up the scale</h2>

You can watch this rating system tighten in real time by following a
single popular speaker line's generations: the **JBL Flip 5** launched
rated **IPX7** (water resistance only); the **Flip 6** moved to a full
**IP67** rating (adding certified dust-tightness); the **Flip 7** moved
further to **IP68**. That progression reflects a broader industry trend
— manufacturers increasingly test and certify the dust digit as well,
recognizing how often portable speakers actually end up at beaches and
campsites where sand and grit are as real a hazard as water.

<h2 id="fine-print">The fine print every rating shares</h2>

Regardless of which specific rating a speaker carries, a few limitations
apply across the entire IP system that are easy to miss:

- **All standard IP water testing uses fresh, still water.** Saltwater
  and chlorinated pool water are more corrosive to seals and metal
  components over time; a device rated for fresh-water submersion isn't
  certified against ocean or pool exposure, even though it will usually
  survive briefly. Rinsing with fresh water after saltwater or pool use
  is the standard mitigation.
  <br /><br />
- **IP ratings test physical ingress, not device function underwater.**
  A submersion-rated speaker surviving a dunk doesn't mean Bluetooth
  connectivity works underwater — radio signals don't propagate well
  through water regardless of the housing's water-tightness.
  <br /><br />
- **Oil, solvents, and other non-water liquids aren't covered.** An IP67
  rating says nothing about resistance to oil, sunscreen, or cleaning
  chemicals.

<h2 id="self-certification">Self-certified vs. third-party verified</h2>

IP testing follows a defined IEC 60529 procedure, but manufacturers can
either self-certify their own testing or submit units to independent
third-party labs for verification. Both are legitimate paths to a
published rating, but a rating from an established, reputable brand
generally carries more real-world confidence than the same numeric
rating from an unfamiliar one, since self-certification relies on the
manufacturer's own testing rigor and honesty.

<h2 id="how-to-shop">How to shop by IP rating</h2>

1. **Match the rating to your actual hazard**, not the highest number
   available — a kitchen or backyard speaker rarely needs IP68-level
   submersion protection; IPX4 or IPX5 comfortably covers splashes and
   rain.
2. **Look for a full two-digit rating (like IP67) specifically if sand
   or dust exposure is realistic** for how you'll use it — an IPX-only
   rating tells you nothing about dust resistance either way.
3. **For IP68 products, check the manufacturer's specific depth/duration
   claim** rather than assuming a standardized figure, since IP68 itself
   doesn't fix one.
4. **Rinse with fresh water after saltwater or pool exposure**,
   regardless of rating, since standard IP testing doesn't certify
   against those specific liquids.

<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>

An IP rating is one of the more genuinely standardized, trustworthy
numbers on a gadget spec sheet — it's a defined engineering test, not a
marketing scale — but it only tells you what was actually tested. Two
digits, tested separately, for two different hazards, with real
limitations (fresh water only, no functional guarantee underwater, no
fixed depth for IP68) that don't show up in the rating number itself.
Reading past the headline "waterproof" claim to the actual two digits is
the difference between choosing the right speaker for a poolside
afternoon and choosing one that's actually built for it.
