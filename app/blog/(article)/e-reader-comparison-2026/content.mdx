<h2 id="why-weeks-not-hours">Why e-readers last weeks, not hours</h2>

An E Ink display's battery advantage over an LCD or OLED tablet screen
comes from a genuine, fundamental difference in how the two technologies
work: E Ink only draws power **when the page changes**, physically
rearranging charged pigment particles to form the new page, then holding
that image with **no ongoing power draw** until the next page turn. An
LCD or OLED screen, by contrast, has to continuously refresh and
backlight or self-illuminate every pixel for as long as it's displaying
anything at all.

That's the entire reason entry-level Kindles and Kobos are commonly
rated for **weeks** of battery life (Kindle Paperwhite and Kindle Scribe
are both rated up to 12 weeks under typical use), while a similarly
priced tablet running the same ebook app lasts roughly **10 hours** on a
charge. It's not a battery-capacity difference — it's a fundamentally
different display technology.

<h2 id="color-eink-tradeoff">The color E Ink trade-off nobody puts on the box clearly</h2>

Color E Ink panels (marketed as E Ink Kaleido 3, used across Kindle
Colorsoft and Kobo's Clara Colour/Libra Colour lines) work by layering a
color filter over the same underlying black-and-white E Ink technology
— and that layering has a real resolution cost that's easy to miss in
marketing copy:

| Mode | Typical resolution |
|---|---|
| Black & white text | 300 PPI |
| Color content (covers, highlights, color notes) | 150 PPI |

Color content on these screens renders at **half the pixel density** of
black-and-white text on the same physical panel. That's not a defect —
it's how the color filter layer works — but it means a color e-reader's
actual text-reading sharpness for standard novels is unaffected (still
300 PPI), while covers, colored highlights, and any color-illustrated
content will look visibly softer than the crisp black-and-white text
around them.

<Callout kind="verified" title="Not a marketing shortcut, a real physical trade-off">
Color E Ink panels also typically display around 4,096 distinct colors
— vivid enough for book covers and basic annotation color-coding, but
nowhere near the color range of an LCD or OLED tablet screen. If color
accuracy for detailed images matters more than paper-like reading
comfort and battery life, a tablet remains the better tool for that
specific job.
</Callout>

<h2 id="ecosystem-lock-in">The real long-term decision: file format lock-in</h2>

The more consequential difference between the two major ecosystems isn't
screen tech at all — it's which file formats and bookstores you're
committing to:

- **Kindle** uses Amazon's own formats (KFX, MOBI) natively. EPUB files
  — the format used by most independent bookstores, public libraries,
  and non-Amazon purchases — have to be converted before they'll display
  properly, which can affect complex formatting in technical books,
  textbooks, or heavily illustrated titles.
- **Kobo** natively supports EPUB, meaning books bought from independent
  stores, sideloaded personal files, and many library-borrowed titles
  work without a conversion step.
- **Library borrowing** differs too: Kindle devices support multiple
  simultaneous library cards through the Libby app, while Kobo devices
  are generally limited to one active library card at a time — the
  opposite of the format-openness pattern, and worth checking against
  your own library habits specifically.

<h2 id="waterproofing">Waterproofing isn't universal, even within one brand's lineup</h2>

It's a common assumption that "e-readers are waterproof" as a category,
but rated water resistance (commonly IPX8 certification) is a
**model-specific feature**, not a category-wide standard. Kobo's Clara
Colour and Libra Colour carry IPX8 certification; Amazon's entry-level,
non-Paperwhite Kindle models have historically shipped **without** water
resistance at all. Checking the specific model's IP rating — not
assuming it from the brand or category — matters if poolside or bathtub
reading is part of your actual use case.

<h2 id="spec-table">Spec-for-spec: entry-level to premium</h2>

| Tier | Example | Screen | Storage | Battery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entry | Amazon Kindle (base model) | 6" E Ink, 300 PPI | 16GB | Up to 4 weeks |
| Mid-range | Kobo Libra Colour | 7" Kaleido 3 color | 32GB | ~14 hours screen-on time |
| Premium (note-taking) | Kindle Scribe | Larger E Ink panel | 16–64GB | Up to 12 weeks |

<Callout kind="caveat" title="Battery figures aren't directly comparable across the table above">
"Weeks of battery" and "hours of screen-on time" are two different
measurement conventions used across different manufacturer spec sheets —
generally, weeks-based figures assume typical daily reading with the
screen mostly idle between page turns, while hours-based figures measure
continuous active screen time. Don't read a "14 hours" spec as worse than
a "12 weeks" spec without checking which measurement convention each
uses.
</Callout>

<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to actually choose</h2>

1. **If you mostly read novels and nonfiction without much color
   content**, the resolution trade-off of color E Ink won't matter —
   prioritize battery life and screen size instead.
2. **If most of your books come from one major retailer already**, let
   that ecosystem decide the brand — converting an entire personal
   library after the fact is the more painful mistake to make.
3. **If you borrow from public libraries regularly**, check both the
   file-format and simultaneous-library-card behavior against your own
   library's specific system before choosing.
4. **If poolside/bath reading is a real use case**, verify the specific
   model's IP rating directly — don't assume it from the product
   category or brand name.

<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>

E-ink's weeks-long battery life is a genuine, physics-based advantage
over any backlit tablet screen, and color E Ink's resolution trade-off is
a real but narrow limitation that mostly affects covers and highlights,
not body text. The decision that actually locks you in for years,
though, is ecosystem: which file formats and bookstore your device
prefers natively — and that's worth more research than the screen
spec most buyers focus on first.
