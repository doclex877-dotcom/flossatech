<h2 id="the-sticker-price-trap">The sticker price doesn't include what you'll actually need</h2>

Apple's entry-level iPad (11-inch, A16 chip) starts around **$299** — a
price genuinely competitive with mid-tier Android tablets. But an iPad
in Apple's lineup **doesn't ship with a stylus or a keyboard case**
included. If your use case involves note-taking or regular typing —
common reasons to buy a tablet rather than just use a phone — you're
adding an **Apple Pencil Pro (~$129)** and, for serious typing, a
**Magic Keyboard (~$299 or more)** on top of the tablet's own price:
over **$400 in accessories** before you have a fully functional
note-taking or productivity setup.

<h2 id="whats-in-the-box">What's actually in the box, compared</h2>

| | Entry iPad (11", A16) | Typical mid-range Android tablet |
|---|---|---|
| Base price | ~$299 | $250–550 depending on model |
| Stylus included | No — Apple Pencil Pro sold separately (~$129) | Often yes on mid-range and up (e.g. Galaxy Tab S10 FE, OnePlus Pad Go 2) |
| Keyboard case included | No — Magic Keyboard sold separately (~$299+) | Varies by model; more commonly bundled or sold as a cheaper accessory |
| microSD expandable storage | No | Common on Android tablets, absent on essentially all iPads |

This is the most consistently overlooked part of the price comparison:
Android tablet manufacturers frequently bundle the accessories that
Apple treats as premium add-ons, which changes the real total cost of
ownership meaningfully in Android's favor for buyers who need a
stylus or keyboard from day one.

<h2 id="display-gap">Where budget Android tablets genuinely fall behind</h2>

The comparison isn't one-sided. At the true budget end specifically,
Android tablets consistently lag on display quality: **60Hz LCD panels**
are common on budget Android models, described by independent reviewers
as roughly **one to two years behind** the panel quality Apple ships even
on its mid-tier iPad Air line. This is where Apple's smaller number of
SKUs works in its favor — with fewer tablet models to differentiate,
Apple doesn't ship a genuinely bad display on any current iPad, while
the wide spread of Android tablet price points means the cheapest
options do cut display quality specifically to hit a lower price.

<h2 id="app-ecosystem">The app ecosystem gap is real and specific</h2>

Beyond hardware, tablet-optimized software remains a genuine iPad
advantage: several category-leading creative and productivity apps —
**Procreate, LumaFusion, Final Cut Pro** — are iPad-exclusive or
meaningfully better optimized on iPadOS than their Android equivalents.
Many Android tablet apps are, in practice, phone apps stretched to a
larger screen rather than built for tablet-sized interfaces from the
ground up. If illustration, video editing, or tablet-native productivity
work is the primary use case, this is a harder gap to close with
hardware specs alone.

<h2 id="update-longevity">Update longevity: the case for paying more up front</h2>

Apple's tablet software-support track record is a genuine differentiator
worth pricing into a purchase decision: a 2020 iPad Pro was still
receiving the current iPadOS version with full security patches as of
2026 — a six-plus-year support window that few Android tablets, even
flagship ones, currently match or explicitly commit to in writing.

<Callout kind="verified" title="Update commitments vary widely by Android manufacturer">
Unlike Apple's consistent cross-lineup support window, Android tablet
update commitments vary significantly by manufacturer and even by model
tier within the same brand — checking the specific stated update policy
for an exact model, rather than assuming iPad-like longevity, is worth
doing before treating a budget Android tablet as a multi-year purchase.
</Callout>

<h2 id="real-price-comparison">A real total-cost comparison</h2>

| Setup | Tablet | + Stylus | + Keyboard | Real total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| iPad, fully equipped | ~$299 | +$129 | +$299 | ~$727 |
| Mid-range Android tablet with stylus + keyboard included | $449–550 | Included | Often included or low-cost add-on | $449–550 |

That gap narrows or disappears entirely if you don't need a stylus or
keyboard at all — for pure media consumption and light browsing, the
bare $299 iPad remains genuinely competitive on price. The comparison
only swings meaningfully once accessories enter the picture.

<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to actually choose</h2>

1. **If you need a stylus or keyboard from day one**, price the full
   iPad setup including accessories before comparing to an Android
   tablet's bundled price — the gap is larger than the base sticker
   prices suggest.
2. **If display quality matters and your budget is genuinely tight**,
   be specifically cautious of the cheapest Android tablets' 60Hz LCD
   panels — this is where budget Android hardware most consistently
   cuts corners.
3. **If your work depends on a specific creative app**, check whether it
   has a genuine, well-reviewed tablet-native version on Android before
   assuming feature parity with the iPad version.
4. **If you plan to keep the tablet for many years**, weight Apple's
   demonstrated update track record against the specific, model-level
   update commitment (if any) published for the Android tablet you're
   considering — don't assume either outcome by default.

<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>

The entry iPad's headline price is genuinely competitive with budget
Android tablets, but that comparison only holds for buyers who won't
need a stylus or keyboard — the moment those accessories enter the
picture, Apple's à la carte pricing model changes the real total cost
substantially, while several Android manufacturers bundle the same
hardware into their sticker price. Budget Android tablets do lag
specifically on display quality and, for creative work, on
tablet-optimized software — real, hardware- and ecosystem-level
trade-offs rather than reasons to dismiss either platform outright.
