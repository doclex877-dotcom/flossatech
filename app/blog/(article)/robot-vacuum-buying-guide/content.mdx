<h2 id="pa-arms-race">The Pa arms race</h2>

If you shopped for a robot vacuum in 2020, a strong model advertised around
2,000 pascals (Pa) of suction. Shop today and you'll see listings for
18,000Pa, 22,000Pa, even the Dreame X60 Max Ultra's advertised **35,000Pa**
and the Ecovacs T90 Pro Omni's **30,000Pa**. That's roughly a
**15-to-17-fold increase in the advertised number** in six years — and
nothing about how a robot vacuum physically works has changed anywhere
near that dramatically.

That gap between the marketing number and the underlying engineering is
the whole story of this article: Pa is a real, measurable unit of
pressure, brands aren't fabricating it, and it still isn't a reliable way
to predict which robot vacuum will actually clean your floor better.

<h2 id="what-pa-measures">What a pascal actually measures</h2>

A pascal is the SI unit of pressure — one newton of force per square
meter. Standard atmospheric pressure is about **101,325 Pa**. When a robot
vacuum is rated at, say, 6,000Pa, that means its motor creates a
pressure differential of roughly 6% relative to atmospheric pressure
inside its airflow path. Outside air rushes in to fill that low-pressure
zone, carrying dust and debris with it into the dustbin.

That's a real physical mechanism, and a higher number does mean the motor
is creating a stronger pressure gap, all else being equal. The problem is
that "all else" is almost never equal between two different robot
vacuums.

<h2 id="the-catch">The catch: it's self-reported, and measured in the wrong place</h2>

Two structural problems undercut the usefulness of Pa as a shopping spec:

1. **There's no standardized test protocol.** Unlike, say, a phone's
   USB-PD wattage rating (governed by the USB-IF's certification
   process), robot vacuum Pa figures are self-reported by each
   manufacturer, measured under conditions that vary brand to brand.
2. **It's measured at the motor, not at the floor.** The pressure a robot
   vacuum's motor generates internally is meaningfully higher than what
   actually reaches the nozzle at the floor — industry commentary
   generally puts the real-world gap at roughly **20–40% lower** than the
   advertised figure, lost to airflow-path resistance, filter condition,
   and seal quality between the brush housing and the floor.

<Callout kind="verified" title="This isn't a brand accusation">
Every major manufacturer — Roborock, Ecovacs, Eufy, Dreame — uses the same
Pa convention and the same lack of a shared third-party test standard.
It's an industry-wide labeling gap, not a specific company cutting
corners.
</Callout>

<h2 id="the-flooring-tiers">How much Pa you actually need, by flooring type</h2>

Despite the inflation at the high end, there's a genuinely useful rule of
thumb buried in the numbers — what floor type actually needs how much
suction:

| Flooring situation | Roughly enough Pa | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bare hardwood, tile, LVP | 1,500–3,000 Pa | Suction matters less here than brush and edge-cleaning design |
| Low-pile carpet, light dust | 4,000–6,000 Pa | Comfortable mid-range for most homes |
| Medium carpet, occasional pet hair | 6,000–8,000 Pa | Where suction starts mattering more than marketing |
| Thick carpet, heavy pet hair | 10,000 Pa or more | Diminishing returns still apply — brush design matters just as much |

Notice that every tier tops out well below the 18,000–35,000Pa figures
now common in flagship marketing. Once you're past roughly 8,000–10,000Pa
of genuine, honestly-measured suction, you're almost certainly buying
headroom you won't use on suction alone — the bigger differentiator at
the top of the market has shifted to navigation, obstacle avoidance, and
self-emptying dock reliability.

<h2 id="the-roomba-counterexample">The counterexample that breaks the whole spec sheet</h2>

The clearest evidence that Pa isn't the whole story: iRobot's Roomba Combo
j9+ **doesn't publish a Pa rating at all**, and continues to earn strong
marks in independent reviews on the strength of its dual multi-surface
rubber brush-roll design rather than raw motor pressure. A well-designed
rubber brush roll extracting embedded dirt consistently can outperform a
poorly designed bristle brush paired with a much higher Pa figure,
because the brush does the majority of the physical debris-lifting work —
the motor's job is to move air fast enough to carry away what the brush
already dislodged.

<Callout kind="caveat" title="A pressure number can't describe a brush">
Think of Pa like horsepower on a car spec sheet: a real, meaningful
number, but one that tells you almost nothing about handling,
transmission, or tire grip — the factors that actually determine how the
car performs on a specific road.
</Callout>

<h2 id="what-independent-testing-checks">What independent testing actually checks</h2>

Because Pa alone is unreliable, the outlets that do publish their own
testing methodology (rather than relaying manufacturer marketing copy)
tend to measure a consistent set of real-world outcomes instead. Vacuum
Wars, one of the more detailed independent testing operations in this
category, publishes results across categories including:

- **Sand/debris removal percentage** on hard flooring
- **Embedded pet hair removal percentage** on carpet, benchmarked against
  a stated category average (their published average sits around 82% for
  embedded pet hair removal on carpet, against which specific models are
  compared)
- **Anti-tangle performance** — the percentage of test runs where hair
  wrapped around the brush roll
- **Obstacle avoidance**, scored against a fixed number of test objects
  (their standard test uses 24 placed objects)

Models that test well tend to score strongly across *several* of these
categories, not just suction — which is a useful signal in itself: a
product that only leads on the one self-reported, unstandardized spec
(Pa) while independent testers don't single it out on pickup performance
is a pattern worth noticing.

<h2 id="reading-a-listing">How to read a robot vacuum listing</h2>

Given all of this, here's a more useful order of operations than sorting
listings by Pa:

1. **Match suction tier to your actual flooring**, using the table above —
   most homes don't need to shop above the 8,000–10,000Pa-equivalent
   range once you account for real-world losses.
2. **Check brush roll design and material**, not just suction. Rubber or
   hybrid rubber/bristle brush rolls generally resist pet-hair tangling
   better than pure bristle designs.
3. **Look for independently tested pickup percentages**, not
   manufacturer-claimed Pa, when comparing two specific models head to
   head.
4. **Treat "self-emptying dock" and "mop" features as separate purchase
   decisions** — they add convenience and cost, but don't affect the
   vacuum's core suction/brush performance.

<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>

Pa ratings on robot vacuums aren't fake, but they're also not a fair
basis for comparison shopping — they're self-reported, measured at the
motor rather than the floor, and only tell part of the mechanical story.
The more reliable signal is matching a genuinely modest suction tier to
your actual flooring, then evaluating brush design and independently
tested pickup rates for the specific models you're choosing between. A
robot vacuum's spec sheet headline number is, at this point, closer to a
car's horsepower figure than a guarantee of how clean your floor will
actually be.
